In 1018, at a banquet in the capital, a man recited a poem. This world is mine, he said, like the full moon with nothing missing. He wasn't the emperor. He was the emperor's grandfather.

The Heian period begins in 794, when the capital moved to Heian-kyo, and it lasts about four hundred years.

Let me give you the answer first. For those four hundred years, the person really running the country was not the emperor himself. It was the emperor's grandfather. First a grandfather on the mother's side, then one on the father's side, and then once again, a grandfather on the mother's side. The face in that seat changed three times. Let's read the Heian period through those three grandfathers.

A New Capital: Heian-kyo (794)

Heian-kyo, today's Kyoto, was founded by Emperor Kanmu. In Nara, the temples had meddled too much in politics, so the new capital allowed no temples except To-ji and Sai-ji, the two guarding its main gate.

Buddhism moved out to the mountains. In 806, Saicho founded the Tendai school on Mount Hiei, and in 816, Kukai founded the Shingon school on Mount Koya.

The First Grandfather: The Fujiwara Regents (866)

The first grandfather was the Fujiwara family. Marry a daughter to the emperor, and make her son the next emperor. While the emperor is still a child, his grandfather governs in his place. That role is called regent. In 866, Fujiwara no Yoshifusa became the first regent from outside the imperial family.

Around this time, in 894, the scholar Sugawara no Michizane proposed stopping the official missions to Tang China, and contact with China grew thin. As a result, a culture of Japan's own grew, including the kana script. Michizane himself was later driven out of the capital by the Fujiwara.

The family reached its peak with Fujiwara no Michinaga, the man with the full-moon poem. He became the grandfather of three emperors, and his son Yorimichi served as regent and chief adviser for about fifty years. But Yorimichi's daughter never gave birth to a prince. If you can't become the grandfather, the whole system stops.

Meanwhile, rebellions broke out far from the capital. In 935, Taira no Masakado rose in the Kanto region, and around the same time, Fujiwara no Sumitomo rose on the Seto Inland Sea. The warrior families who put them down, the Taira and the Minamoto, grew stronger.

The Second Grandfather: Cloistered Rule (1086)

Once an emperor came along without Fujiwara blood, the imperial family moved to take politics back. In 1086, Emperor Shirakawa handed the throne to his son, became a retired emperor, and kept running the government anyway. This is called cloistered rule. He kept governing into his grandson's reign.

Picture a CEO who steps down, then keeps running the company as chairman of the board.

The Third Grandfather: Taira no Kiyomori (1156 to 1185)

The warriors who guarded the retired emperors soon became the stars of the court's own fights. Through the Hogen Rebellion in 1156 and the Heiji Rebellion in 1159, Taira no Kiyomori defeated Minamoto no Yoshitomo.

Kiyomori rose all the way to Grand Minister of State and married his daughter to the emperor. Their son became Emperor Antoku. Kiyomori had taken the same seat the Fujiwara once held: the mother's father.

Kiyomori wasn't so much the man who opened a new age as the man who copied the Heian way better than anyone. And that's exactly why he drew the same resentment the nobles had. In 1180, members of the imperial family and the Minamoto rose up against the Taira, and in 1185, the Taira were destroyed at Dan-no-ura.

The Heian Period in One Line

The Fujiwara, the retired emperors, and Taira no Kiyomori. As these three grandfathers took turns, the Heian period moved through its four hundred years. And the ones who finally took the seat were warriors who never needed to be anyone's grandfather.

Here's a question for you. If you were a powerful noble in Heian Japan, would you rather be the emperor, or the emperor's grandfather?
