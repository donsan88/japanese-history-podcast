In 1185, a grandmother took her six-year-old grandson, the emperor of Japan, in her arms and leapt into the sea. Only a few years earlier, her family had stood at the very top of the country.

The Genpei War comes at the very end of the Heian period. From 1180 to 1185, just six years. In that short time, the Taira, the family at the top of Japan, vanished into the sea.

Let me give you the answer first. The Taira weren't brought down by a single hero. They were brought down by four challengers, each holding a different trump card. A prince with a written order, a general who seized the capital, a genius of the battlefield, and a man who never once stood on the front line. Let's follow the Genpei War through those four, in order.

The Taira at the Top (1156 to 1179)

First, let's look at the side being challenged. Warriors started out as lowly guards for the nobility. Among them, two families grew strong: the Minamoto, based in the east, and the Taira, based in the west.

The turning points were the Hogen Rebellion in 1156 and the Heiji Rebellion in 1159. A family quarrel between the imperial house and the nobles was settled by warrior power, and in the process, Taira no Kiyomori crushed the Minamoto. But Kiyomori spared the life of Minamoto no Yoritomo, the young son of the Minamoto leader, and sent him away to Izu instead. That act of mercy would one day seal the Taira's fate.

Kiyomori became the first warrior ever to serve as Grand Minister of State, and he married his daughter Tokuko to the emperor. Their son became Emperor Antoku. He also built up Fukuhara, in today's Kobe, as a base for overseas trade.

But the nobles' resentment kept growing, and a plot to overthrow the Taira was even uncovered at Shishigatani in Kyoto. When the court tried to take away Taira lands, Kiyomori marched his troops into the capital, and in 1179, he locked Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa inside his residence. It looked as if no one could stand against the Taira anymore.

The First Challenger: Prince Mochihito (1180)

The first challenger was Prince Mochihito, a son of Go-Shirakawa. His trump card wasn't a sword. It was paper: an order calling on Minamoto warriors across the country to strike down the Taira.

Kiyomori sent troops at once. The prince fled from temple to temple, heading for Kofuku-ji in Nara, but he was caught near the Byodo-in at Uji, hit by an arrow, and killed. His challenge was over in a matter of months.

But the order didn't stop. Minamoto warriors scattered across the provinces rose up one after another. Yoritomo in Izu raised an army too, and at the Battle of Fujigawa, he drove back the Taira forces.

Kiyomori struck back by burning the temples of Nara that had sided with the prince. But in 1181, Kiyomori fell ill with a high fever and died. At the most critical moment, the Taira lost their pillar.

The Second Challenger: Minamoto no Yoshinaka (1183)

The second challenger was Minamoto no Yoshinaka, who raised an army in today's Nagano. His trump card was momentum. In 1183, he smashed a huge Taira army at the Battle of Kurikara Pass and raced toward Kyoto ahead of everyone else.

The Taira saw him coming, abandoned the capital, and fled west. What matters here is what they took with them: the child emperor Antoku, and the Three Sacred Treasures. The Three Sacred Treasures are a mirror, a sword, and a curved jewel. Whoever holds them is recognized as the rightful emperor. They are the symbols of the throne.

Think of the British Crown Jewels as the very proof of a monarch's right to rule. Now imagine the side carrying them is the one running away. Chase them, and you risk becoming a rebel who raises his bow against the emperor. Neither Yoshinaka nor Go-Shirakawa could go after the Taira head-on.

On top of that, once Yoshinaka's army entered the capital, it started looting. When Go-Shirakawa moved to drive Yoshinaka out, Yoshinaka attacked him instead and locked him up yet again. The man who had taken the capital had lost the capital's trust. In 1184, Yoshinaka was killed by an army Yoritomo sent, led by Yoshitsune and others.

The Third Challenger: Minamoto no Yoshitsune (1184 to 1185)

The third challenger was Yoritomo's younger brother, Minamoto no Yoshitsune. His trump card was a way of fighting no one else could imagine.

In 1184 came the Battle of Ichi-no-tani. The Taira had dug in with the sea at their backs. Yoshitsune rode his horses straight down the steep cliffs behind them and charged into their camp. Like Hannibal crossing the Alps to strike Rome, he came from the one direction nobody guarded. The Taira fled by sea and moved their base to Yashima, on the island of Shikoku.

In 1185 came the Battle of Yashima. Yoshitsune deliberately set sail into a stormy sea and caught the Taira off guard. This is also the battle where the master archer Nasu no Yoichi hit a fan swaying on a boat, with a single arrow.

And in that same year, the final stage was Dan-no-ura, a strait between today's Yamaguchi and Fukuoka, where the tidal current changes direction. The Taira, experienced at sea, held the upper hand at first. But a Taira naval force switched sides to the Minamoto, and the tide of battle turned all at once.

Knowing they had lost, the Taira threw themselves into the sea. Kiyomori's widow took the young Emperor Antoku in her arms, along with the sacred sword, and sank beneath the waves. And so the family that had reached the very top came to its end.

The Last Challenger: Minamoto no Yoritomo

The last challenger was Yoritomo, the very boy whose life Kiyomori had spared.

For six years, Yoritomo barely left Kamakura. He sent his brothers to the battlefield, while he himself pulled the eastern warriors together and kept deciding who would be trusted with which lands.

This is the most important thing to take from the Genpei War. Prince Mochihito with his written order, Yoshinaka who took the capital, Yoshitsune who won battle after battle: none of them stayed in power to the end. The only one left standing was Yoritomo, the man who held the system that protected the warriors' livelihoods. Winning battles and winning an age turned out to be two different things.

The Genpei War in One Line

An order, a capital, a battlefield, and a system. Four challengers passed the baton one after another, and in six years, the Taira were gone.

Here's a question for you. Would you rather be Yoshitsune, who won every battle, or Yoritomo, who never left Kamakura?
